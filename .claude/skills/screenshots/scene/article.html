Field notes

Walking the coastal path

Day four to day nine · 112 km · Long read

The morning started grey and soft, the way most mornings do on this stretch of coast. By the time the kettle had boiled, the fog had lifted off the water and the headland across the bay came into view, one ridge at a time, as if someone were slowly turning up the light.

We had planned to be on the path by eight. We were on it by half past nine, which on this trip counted as a good start. The first hour climbs steadily through birch and heather, and nobody talks much. You find a rhythm, or you don't, and the hill decides which.

Day four: the long ridge

From the top of the first climb the path follows the ridge for almost eleven kilometres. It is the easiest walking of the whole route and the most exposed. On a clear day you can see three islands to the west and the white line of the ferry crossing between them. On a windy day you see mostly your own boots.

We had both kinds of day before lunch. The wind came in from the sea around eleven, hard enough to lean on, and dropped again an hour later as if it had changed its mind. In the quiet afterwards, we could hear sheep somewhere below us, and the faint knock of a boat engine far out on the water.

Lunch was bread, cheese, two apples and the last of the chocolate, eaten behind a cairn that someone had built with real care. Each stone sat flat on the one below. We added one of our own before moving on, which is the rule, or at least the habit.

Day five: down to the harbour

The descent to the harbour is short and steep, and it is where most people feel the walk in their knees. The path zigzags through old pasture, past stone walls that have long since stopped keeping anything in, and comes out beside a row of red boathouses.

The village has one shop, one café and one bench with a view, and on a Tuesday afternoon all three were open. We sat on the bench for longer than we meant to. A fishing boat came in, tied up and unloaded in less time than it took us to finish a cup of coffee.

“You walk the coast to see the sea,” the woman in the shop told us, “and then you spend the whole time looking at your feet.”

She was right, of course. That evening we made a point of stopping every half hour, putting the packs down and just looking. It slowed us down by about forty minutes. It was the best forty minutes of the day.

Day six: rain, and then more rain

There is not much to say about day six except that it rained, and that the rain was very thorough. It found its way into jacket cuffs, map pockets and the inside of a supposedly waterproof bag. We walked fourteen kilometres and saw almost none of them.

What we did see was close up: water running in silver threads down the rock, the bright orange of lichen, a frog sitting perfectly still in the middle of the path as if it had been waiting for us. Bad weather makes the world smaller, and in a way, more interesting.

Day seven: the lighthouse

The lighthouse sits at the end of a long finger of rock, and the path out to it is only open at low tide. We timed it badly and had to wait two hours on the near side, which turned out to be no hardship at all. The sun came out. The rock dried. We slept.

When the water finally drew back, the crossing took ten minutes over wet stone and seaweed. The keeper's cottage is now a small museum, run by volunteers, with a guest book going back forty years. Most entries mention the wind. A surprising number mention the cake.

Days eight and nine: the way home

The last two days follow the old post road inland, away from the sea and through farmland. It is gentle walking, and after a week on the coast it felt almost strange to be surrounded by fields and fences and the sound of tractors.

On the final evening we climbed a small hill behind the last village, just to see the sea one more time. It was flat and silver and very far away. We stood there until the light went, and then we walked down to the bus stop, and the trip was over.
